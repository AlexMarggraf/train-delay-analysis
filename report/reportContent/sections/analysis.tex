\section{Analysis}
For the analysis, we wanted to see the differences in train delays in Switzerland and Germany
and how much they are effected by the weather in those countries.
First, we examined the average train delay in each country.
We then analyzed how weather conditions affected the mean delay.
We also did a statistical analysis to properly interpret these results.
The next step was to further split the data to obtain more detailed result.
So we checked how a specific weather impacted the average delay.
Since our datasets did not explicitly indicate the specific weather present at a given time, we had to take another approach for this.
Both datasets, the Swiss weather dataset and the German data set measured precipitation in mm which is an indicator for the weather.
Therefore, the table shown in \Cref{fig:g5_prec_to_weather} was used to map the precipitation levels to weather categories.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        weather type & dry & light rain & rain & heavy rain & snow \\
        \hline
        precipitation & 0 & 0 - 5 & 5 - 10 & > 10 & > 0 with temp < 0\\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Table used to map precipitation to weather}
    \label{fig:g5_prec_to_weather}
\end{figure}
We also looked at the impact of each precipitation on the average delay using precipitation intervals of 5 mm.
Since we already took a look oon the impact of the weather, we also wanted to examine whether there is a correlation
between train delays and the month of the year.
Our datasets covered the period from April 2024 to September 2025,
so we looked for all the months in that time span.
We also went a step further and checked the delays and cancellations for the individual Cantons and Counties
with respect to the total train stops.

\subsection*{Queries used for the analysis}

In order to get proper results and functioning queries we had to take a look at the datasets themselves.
Here we noticed that the Swiss train data set had train stations from different countries and from Germany in.
In the analysis, this would have led to some problems since some german stations would appear twice with different delays.
In order to fix this issue, we modified the final database such that we can check if the train station comes from the Swiss or German data set.
With this change, we could get more meaningful results.
For the average delay and cancellations per Canton and County, we used geopy to map each train station to the corresponding Canton and County.\\
In order to get the data from the database, we always divided the query into four smaller queries.
One for each dataset, the German train and weather data and one for the Swiss train and weather data.
For the analysis of the average delay per Canton and County, we moved some of the logic and calculations into the query since they were not too complex.
Here we also had to keep in mind to write a query which checks the German train stops in the Swiss train data set and add them to the German data.
In order to keep the queries a bit more simple, we also added a filter to it to the query more readable.\\
For the average delay per country, we kept the logic out of the queries since this would have led to much greater running time of our code.
So here we kept the queries as simple as possible and did all the calculations with the data in the code itself.\\
Taking a closer look at the results we also noticed that some delays were negative and some were absurdly large.
Here we had to adjust a train with negative delay, which means that it was arriving too early to a delay of 0 and
the large outliers had the problem that the time stamps got mixed up so we subtracted 1440 minutes form them which led to accurate results.

\subsection*{Visualisation methods used for the analysis}

For the visualisation of the results form the average delay per Canton and County, we used the package choropleth maps.
With this, we could easily display the results in a clean and readable format.
This meant that we also had to get some
GeoJSON files for Switzerland, Germany, Cantons and Counties.
For the display of the impact of the weather and the precipitation on the average delay and how the average delay changes per month,
we used bar charts. To create the charts, we used matplotlib and numpy.
To be able to make calculations with such big data, we also used dataframes from pandas.
With these approaches, we were able to get a clean and readable analysis with readable and efficient code.

\subsection*{Results of the analysis}

In this section, we now take a closer look at the final results of our analysis of the average delay for each country, Cantons and Counties
and how much the weather effected the average delay in Switzerland and Germany.

\subsubsection*{Delay per Country and the effect of the weather}\phantom{}\\
The first thing we chacked is the delay per county in order to get a solid baseline and to check if the data is reasonable.
Here we calculated the average delay in minutes, the median delay, the standard deviation of delays and the actual count of train stops.
\Cref{fig:g5_country_delay} shows our results, we rounded to the second decimal place and the delay is calculated in minutes.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        country & mean delay & median delay & sd. of delays & count of train stops\\
        \hline
        Switzerland & 1.06 & 0.63 & 2.94 & 40137204 \\
        \hline
        Germany & 4.52 & 2.00 & 9.96 & 3391881 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{This table shows the mean delay per country}
    \label{fig:g5_country_delay}
\end{figure}
Looking at the results of this figure, we notice that first of all, the Swiss data set has much more train stops and is therefore
much more detailed and accurate.
We also notice that in Germany, we should expect that any train has 3.46 minutes more delay than a train in Switzerland.
This means that the trains in Switzerland are way more on time than in Germany. \newline \newline
Since we have a solid baseline now, we now checked the impact of the weather on the average delay.
For this we said that normal weather means that the precipitation is smaller than 5 and bad weather means that the precipitation is greater or equal to 5.
To get the actual effect now, we subtract the average delay for normal weather from the average delay from bad weather.
With this, we get the actual effect on the average delay.
The results of this calculation are shown in \Cref{fig:g5_weather_delay}, the delays are shown in minutes and rounded to the second decimal place.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        country & delay normal weather & delay bad weather & effect of the weather \\
        \hline
        Switzerland & 1.06 & 1.33 & 0.27 \\
        \hline
        Germany & 4.52 & 5.23 & 1.01 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{This figure shows the effect of the weather on the average delay}
    \label{fig:g5_weather_delay}
\end{figure}
In this figure we see that in Switzerland, a train is expected to have a delay of 0.27 minutes more with bad weather than with normal weather.
For germany, we should expect that the delay increases by 1.01 minutes if the weather is bad.
This shows that the impact of the weather on the German train net
is much greater than on the Swiss train net.
This also means that even with bad weather, the Swiss trains are much more on time than German trains
for each weather. \newline \newline
The datasets differ very much in their size and the actual count of delays per normal weather and bad weather also differ very much.
In order to make more accurate conclusions, we also ran a Mann-Whitney-test \cite{mann1947test} for Germany and Switzerland for the normal and bad weather.
\Cref{fig:g5_Mann_Whitney_test} shows the results of the Mann-Whitney-test.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        country & U-statistic & p-value & n$_{\text{bad\_weather}}$ & n$_{\text{normal\_weather}}$\\
        \hline
        Switzerland & $2.55\times10^{12}$ & 0.0 & 116921 & 40020283\\
        \hline
        Germany & $9.96\times10^{9}$ & $3.6\times10^{-21}$ & 5486 & 3386395\\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{This table shows the results for the Mann-Whitney-test}
    \label{fig:g5_Mann_Whitney_test}
\end{figure}
Here, a larger value of the U-statistic indicates a stronger tendency for delays during bad weather to be higher than during normal weather.
For both countries, we see that the U-statistic value is really large so there is a strong tendency that bad weather increases the delays.
The p-value is an indicator if the hypothesis that bad weather increases the delay is true or not.
The closer this is to 0, the higher is the chance
that the delay is indeed affected by bad weather.
For both countries the p-value is really close to zero which means that we can be sure
that the delays are increased by bad weather.
We also see that the sample size for bad weather is much smaller than for good weather which means that interpreting the results solely based on total counts should be done with caution. \newline \newline
At the end we also made a regression model for our data.
For the regression model, the data from Switzerland was our baseline.
We specified the following regression model to analyze the effect of weather and country on train delays:
\begin{align*}
\text{delay}_i &= \beta_{0} + \beta_{1} \times \text{Precipitation}_i + \beta_{2} \times \text{Temperature}_i \nonumber + \beta_{3} \times \text{WindSpeed}_i\\
    &+ \beta_{4} \times \mathbb{1}(\text{DE}_i) + \beta_{5} \times \Bigl(\text{Precipitation}_i \times \mathbb{1}(\text{DE}_i)\Bigr) + \varepsilon_i
\label{eq:reg_model}
\end{align*}
We created the regression model based on the formula, and we got the results for the variables, shown in \Cref{fig:g5_reg_model}.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        variable & coefficient & standard error & t-value & p-value\\
        \hline
        intercept & 0.995 & 0.001 & 896.866 & 0.000\\
        \hline
        precipitation & 0.085 & 0.001 & 124.823 & 0.000\\
        \hline
        temperature & 0.001 & 7.844 & 15.726 & 0.000\\
        \hline
        wind speed & 0.008 & 0.000 & 45.776 & 0.000\\
        \hline
        country DE & 3.476 & 0.006 & 630.647 & 0.000\\
        \hline
        precipitation: DE & 0.159 & 0.013 & 12.285 & 0.000\\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Variables of the regression model}
    \label{fig:g5_reg_model}
\end{figure}
The coefficient indicates how much the average delay changes when the corresponding variable increases by one unit.
This indicates that precipitation has the strongest impact on train delays and that the temperature and the wind speed have minimal effects on the actual delay.

We have to keep in mind that based on our time span, we are missing the summer months with the best weather.
Based on the consistency of the average delays of the Swiss trains, we do not expect to see a decrease in the average delay in those months.
For Germany, in the summer the average delays might be smaller in the summer since the weather has a much higher impact in Germany and the trains in Germany are much more inconsistent than in Switzerland.

\subsection*{Conclusion of the analysis}

At the end, we can say that the Swiss trains are much more punctual and reliable with an average delay of 1.06 minutes than the German trains with an average delay of 4.52 minutes.
We can also see that the impact of the weather and the month in Switzerland is pretty small and in Germany, the effect is much more noticeable.
This leads to the assumptions that the German train net is much more unreliable and less robust than the Swiss train net.
We can also see that the trains in each Canton are way more reliable than in the Counties of Germany.
The trains in Switzerland are also less likely to be canceled than the German trains. \newline \newline
We have to keep in mind that the Swiss data is much larger than the German data, which means that the Swiss data is way more accurate than the German data.
But the German data is still pretty big which means that the conclusion might still be correct but to get better results, we should take a look on larger datasets from Germany.
What we also have to consider is that there were some outliers in the datasets which have a delay of over one day, which is most likely due to the fact that there was a mistake in the actual timestamp in the datasets.
For these outliers and the trains which arrived earlier than expected, we changed the calculated delay.
Here we might have had wrong assumptions, and we might have had an influence on the actual data, and we might have missed some outliers,
which are still in the results. \newline \newline
In the end, we can conclude that our results are meaningful and consistent with real-world experience.
